It is a well known result in the study of containers \cite{Abbott2005}
that their interpretation \(\ext{-}\) as mappings between types yields a
full and faithful functor from the category \(\Cont\) of containers and
container morphisms, to the category \(\Endo(\Set)\) of \(\Set\)
endofunctors. Moreover, it has been shown \cite{Uustalu2017} that this
functor is strong monoidal when the two categories are equipped with the
monoidal structures of container substitution on one side, and of functor
composition on the other.

Stated in this way, these results apply to the interpretations of type
theory where types stand for sets. However, it is also well known by now
that more complex interpretations are possible and worthwhile. When
attempting to replicate the above reasoning in Homotopy Type Theory
\cite{UnivalentFoundationsProgram2013} (an interpretation in which types stand for
homotopy types), one has to add the constraint that shapes and positions
are set-truncated, lest the full faithfulness result fail to extend.

In this work we adopt a more foundational approach, exploring a higher
categorical setting in which groupoid-truncated containers shall be
rightful inhabitants, where we expect to (and indeed will) find analogous
properties.
% pretty dramatic I know

\begin{section}{Locally Univalent 2,1-Categories}
 
 We deal with the following notion of (2,1)-category, adapted from
 \cite{Finster2021}:
 
 A locally univalent (2,1)-category (henceforth, \lutc{}) is a wild
 category\footnote{In Cubical jargon, this is what you get by naively
	 transcribing the definition of category in MLTT, without requiring the
	 \(\hom\)-types to be \(\hSet\)s. Similarly, one speaks of wild
	 functors and wild natural transformations.} whose \(\hom\)-types are
 \(\hGpd\)s. Explicitly:
 
 \begin{align*}
	 C_0              & : \Type                                                                                                                       \\
	 C_1(X , Y)       & : \hGpd                                          & \forall & X , Y : C_0                                                      \\
	 \id_X            & : C_1(X , X)                                     & \forall & X : C_0                                                          \\
	 f \star g        & : C_1(X , Z)                                     & \forall & X, Y, Z : C_0, \; f : C_1(X, Y), \; g : C_1(Y, Z)                \\
	 \idl_f           & : \id_X \star f \equiv f                         & \forall & X , Y : C_0, f : C_1(X,Y)                                        \\
	 \idr_f           & : f \star \id_Y \equiv f                         & \forall & X , Y : C_0, f : C_1(X,Y)                                        \\
	 \assoc_{f, g, h} & : (f \star g) \star h \equiv f \star (g \star h) & \forall & X , Y , Z , W : C_0,                                             \\
	                  &                                                  &         & f : C_1(X, Y), \; g : C_1(Y, Z), \; h : C_1(Z, W)              
 \end{align*}
 
 These must satisfy:
 
 \begin{itemize}
	 \item The triangle equation: for all appropriately typed \(f, g\),
	       \[ \assoc_{f, \id_Y,g} \ct f \trl \idl_g = \idr_f \trr g \]
	 \item The pentagon equation: for all appropriately typed \(f, g, h, k\),
	       \[
		       \assoc_{f,g,h} \trr k
		       \ct \assoc_{f,g \star h,k}
		       \ct f \trl \assoc_{g,h,k} \
		       = \assoc_{f \star g,h,k}
		       \ct \assoc_{f,g,h \star k}
	       \]
 \end{itemize}
 
 In the above, 2-cell composition \(p \ct q\) is defined as HoTT-style
 path composition, and whiskerings \(f \trl q\) and \(p \trr g\) are
 defined as \eg{} \(f \trl q \defas \cong \, (f \star -) \, q\). Morally,
 we let the identity type \(f \equiv g\) and the associated syntactic
 rules internalise the groupoid structure of a 2,1-category. As it is
 possible to interpret MLTT as a theory of \(\infty\)-groupoids, this
 provides a lot of the constructions and coherences required by higher
 category theory. For example, all functions between types are
 automatically functorial on their identity groupoids. In particular this
 makes 1-cell composition \(- \star -\) bifunctorial, something which
 would be an extra requirement to be imposed on it in a traditional
 presentation.
 
 Suitable notions of pseudofunctors and pseudonatural transformations can
 be adapted (in our case from \cite{Johnson2020}) to this setting. For
 instance, for \((F_0, F_1, F_{\id}, F_{\star})\) a wild functor, \(F_1\) being its
 action on 1-cells, the action on 2-cells is given to us by the theory as
 \(F_2 := \cong F_1\). Some extra coherences are required, but notably
 not \(F_\star\) being natural on 2-cells, which is provable by path
 induction. A similar reasoning allows us to prove naturality of square
 fillers for wild natural transformations.
 
\end{section}

\begin{section}{Groupoid Containers}
 
 A \emph{groupoid container} is a container \(\cont{S}{P}\) such that
 \(S\) is an \(\hGpd\), and so is \(P_s\) for any \(s : S\). Groupoid
 containers with morphisms between them form a \lutc{}, \(\GpdCont\). We
 can now state and prove:
 
 \begin{theorem} The interpretation of containers \(\ext{-}\) yields a full and
	 faithful pseudofunctor from \(\GpdCont\) to the \lutc{} \(\Endo(\HGpd)\) of
	 \(\HGpd\) endopseudofunctors.
 \end{theorem}
 
\end{section}

\begin{section}{Monoids in \(\GpdCont\)}
 
 
 In \cite{Uustalu2017} and \cite{DePascalis2025} we characterise monoids
 in a monoidal category. How does this generalise to our new scope?
 
 \begin{itemize}
	 \item \((\GpdCont, {\csubst}, \I)\) is not strictly associative and unital on objects,
	       but all the 2-cells you might think of are trivial; 
	       
	 \item \((\Endo(\HGpd), ;, \Id)\) is strictly associative and unital on objects, but all higher
	       structure is non-trivial.
 \end{itemize}
 
 The definition encompassing both is that of a monoidal bicategory
 \cite[Section 12.1]{Johnson2020}, which we adapt to the locally
 univalent formulation. In this context we have to pick a suitable
 notion of monoid object. Pseudomonoids in a Gray-monoid are described
 in \cite[Section 3]{Day1997}, and in classical higher category theory
 this definition somehow suffices to define pseudomonoids in a
 monoidal bicategory, since classically, every monoidal bicategory is
 equivalent to a Gray-monoid. Expectably, to meet the constructive
 locally univalent setting, we end up \emph{relaxifying} this
 definition to describe pseudomonoids in \(\GpdCont\). 
 
 Formalisation of this part of locally univalent 2,1-category theory
 is still underway. Nonetheless, we can already apply this definition
 of pseudomonoid in \((\GpdCont, \csubst, \I)\) to replicate the
 combinatorial unpacking seen in \cite{Uustalu2017}. The resulting
 list of data and equations, though perhaps useful for formalisation
 or instance search purposes, are unwieldy for pen and paper
 presentation.
 
\end{section}

\begin{section}{Free monoids in \(\GpdCont\)}
 
 In classical category theory, it is well known that the free monad
 over a polynomial functor is itself polynomial. This translates to
 our setting as well.
 
 \begin{definition}
	 Let \(F \defas \cont{S}{P}\) be a groupoid container. One can define a new
	 container \(F^* \defas \cont{S^*}{P^*}\) by:
	 \begin{align*}
		 S^* & \defas \mu X \; . \; \Unit + \prod_{s : S} (P_s \to X)
		     & P^*_{\inl \, *}                                        & \defas \Unit                                             \\
		     &                                                        & P^*_{\inr \, s\, s'} & \defas \sum_{p : P_s} P*_{s'\, p}
	 \end{align*}
 \end{definition}
 
 This container carries a pseudomonoid structure in the aforementioned
 sense, and it is our working hypothesis that it represents the free
 pseudomonad on \(\ext{F}\), as is the case for set containers.
 Furthermore, we are in the process of proving that it constitutes
 some sort of generalised list object (in the sense of
 \cite{Fiore2017}), for which the following inference rule holds:
 
 \[
	 \begin{prooftree}
		 \hypo{H \Rightarrow G}
		 \hypo{G \csubst F \Rightarrow G}
		 \infer2{H \csubst F^* \Rightarrow G}
	 \end{prooftree}
   \qquad
	 \text{for any fixed container \(H\)}
 \]
 
 Where \(H \Rightarrow G\) denotes container morphisms between \(H\)
 and \(G\).
 
\end{section}
