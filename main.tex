\vspace{1.2em}

Containers~\cite{Abbott2005} are a type-theoretical construct encoding
signatures for a wide class of strictly positive inductive datatypes. A
container \(\cont{S}{P}\) consists of a type \(S\) of \emph{shapes} and an
\(S\)-indexed family of types \(P\). For a shape \(s : S\), \(P_s\) is called
the type of \emph{positions} for \(s\). For a container \(\cont{S}{P}\), a
type-level mapping called its \emph{interpretation} (or \emph{extent}) is
defined, as:

\[
  \ext{\cont{S}{P}} \, X \defas \theext{S}{P}{X}
\]

Least and greatest fixpoints of \(\ext{\cont{S}{P}}\) give semantics for W-types
and M-types, respectively, having \(\cont{S}{P}\) as their signature.

It is a well known result in the study of containers that \(\ext{-}\) extends to
a full and faithful functor from the category \(\Cont\) of containers and
container morphisms, to the category \(\Endo(\Set)\) of \(\Set\) endofunctors.
The so defined full subcategory of \(\Endo(\Set)\) corresponds to a class of
\(\Set\) endofunctors known as \emph{polynomial endofunctors}\footnote{There are
several variants of the notion of polynomial in category theory, differing in
approach and generality, and the same is true for containers. Hence, the overlap
between the two scopes is technically more subtle, though they arguably
represent attempts to describe the same kind of construction in different
theoretical settings. A modern categorical treatment of polynomials can be found
in \cite{Gambino2009}.}. Moreover, it has been shown~\cite{Uustalu2017} that
this functor is strong monoidal when the two categories are equipped with the
monoidal structures of container substitution on one side, and of functor
composition on the other.

Stated in this way, these results apply to the interpretations of type theory
where types stand for sets. However, it is also well known by now that more
complex interpretations are possible and worthwhile. When attempting to
replicate the above reasoning in Homotopy Type
Theory~\cite{UnivalentFoundationsProgram2013} (an interpretation in which types
stand for homotopy types), one has to add the constraint that shapes and
positions are set-truncated, lest the full faithfulness result fail to extend.

In this work we adopt a more foundational approach, exploring a higher
categorical setting in which groupoid-truncated containers shall be rightful
inhabitants, where we expect to (and indeed will) find analogous properties.

\begin{section}{Locally Univalent (2,1)-Categories}

  Bicategories, and in particular locally univalent bicategories, have
  already been studied in a HoTT setting in \cite{Ahrens2021}.
  We chose to work with a different notion of bicategory, or (2,1)-category, adapted from
  ~\cite[Definition 5]{Finster2021}:

  \begin{definition}[\lutc{}]
    A locally univalent (2,1)-category (henceforth, \lutc{}) is a wild
    category\footnote{As described in \cite{Capriotti2017}, this is what you get
    by naively transcribing the definition of category in MLTT, without
    requiring the \(\hom\)-types to be \(\hSet\)s. Similarly, one speaks of wild
    functors and wild natural transformations.} whose \(\hom\)-types are
    \(\hGpd\)s, and whose 2-cells satisfy triangle and pentagon equations.
    Explicitly:

    \begin{align*}
      C_0              & : \Type                                                                                                        \\
      C_1(X , Y)       & : \hGpd                                          & \forall & X , Y : C_0                                       \\
      \id_X            & : C_1(X , X)                                     & \forall & X : C_0                                           \\
      f \star g        & : C_1(X , Z)                                     & \forall & X, Y, Z : C_0, \; f : C_1(X, Y), \; g : C_1(Y, Z) \\
      \idl_f           & : \id_X \star f \equiv f                         & \forall & X , Y : C_0, f : C_1(X,Y)                         \\
      \idr_f           & : f \star \id_Y \equiv f                         & \forall & X , Y : C_0, f : C_1(X,Y)                         \\
      \assoc_{f, g, h} & : (f \star g) \star h \equiv f \star (g \star h) & \forall & X , Y , Z , W : C_0,                              \\
                       &                                                  &         & f : C_1(X, Y), \; g : C_1(Y, Z), \; h : C_1(Z, W)
    \end{align*}

    These must satisfy:

    \begin{itemize}
      \item The triangle equation: for all appropriately typed \(f, g\),
            \[
              \assoc_{f, \id_Y,g} \ct f \trl \idl_g = \idr_f \trr g
            \]
      \item The pentagon equation: for all appropriately typed \(f, g, h, k\),
            \[
              \assoc_{f,g,h} \trr k \ct \assoc_{f,g \star h,k} \ct f \trl \assoc_{g,h,k} \
              = \assoc_{f \star g,h,k} \ct \assoc_{f,g,h \star k}
            \]
    \end{itemize}
  \end{definition}

  In the above, 2-cell composition \(p \ct q\) is defined as HoTT-style path
  composition, and whiskerings \(f \trl q\) and \(p \trr g\) are defined as
  \eg{} \(f \trl q \defas \cong \, (f \star-) \, q\). Morally, we let the
  identity type \(f \equiv g\) and the associated syntactic rules internalise
  the groupoid structure of a (2,1)-category. As it is possible to interpret
  MLTT as a theory of \(\infty\)-groupoids, this provides a lot of the
  constructions and coherences required by higher category theory. For example,
  all functions between types are automatically functorial on their identity
  groupoids. In particular this makes 1-cell composition \(- \star -\)
  bifunctorial, something which would be an extra requirement to be imposed on
  it in a traditional presentation.

  Suitable notions of pseudofunctors and pseudonatural transformations can be
  adapted (in our case from~\cite{Johnson2020}) to this setting. For instance,
  for \((F_0, F_1, F_{\id}, F_{\star})\) a wild functor, \(F_1\) being its
  action on 1-cells, the action on 2-cells is given to us by the theory as
  \(F_2 \defas \cong F_1\). Some extra coherences are required, but notably not
  \(F_\star\) being natural on 2-cells, which is provable by path induction. A
  similar reasoning allows us to prove naturality of square fillers for wild
  natural transformations.

  Some examples of locally univalent (2,1)-categories are:

  \begin{itemize}
    \item \(\HGpd\), whose objects are \(\hGpd\)s;

    \item Wild functors between wild categories \(C\) and \(D\), and
      wild natural transformations between them, provided that \(D\)
      has \lutc{} structure;

    \item Any (pre)category in the HoTT sense, \ie{} any wild category whose
          \(\hom\)-types are \(\hSet\)s.
  \end{itemize}

\end{section}

\begin{section}{Groupoid Containers}

  A \emph{groupoid container} \footnote{Gylterud~\cite{Gylterud2011}
    has also investigated introducing higher structures to containers,
    by first generalising to \emph{categorified containers}, where
    \(S\) is a category and \(P\) is a functor from \(S\) to \(\Cat\),
    and then restricting again to \emph{symmetric containers}, \ie{}
    categorified containers where \(S\) is a groupoid and \(P\) lands
    in the \(\Cat\)-subcategory of discrete categories, hence being an
  \(S\)-indexed families of sets. Our scope is broader, in that we
consider groupoid-indexed families of groupoids.} is a container
\(\cont{S}{P}\) such that \(S\) is an \(\hGpd\), and so is \(P_s\) for
any \(s : S\). Groupoid containers with morphisms between them form a
\lutc{}, \(\GpdCont\). We can now state and prove:

  \begin{theorem}
    The interpretation of containers \(\ext{-}\) yields a full and faithful
    pseudofunctor from \(\GpdCont\) to the \lutc{} \(\Endo(\HGpd)\) of \(\HGpd\)
    endopseudofunctors.
  \end{theorem}

Examples of groupoid containers include:

\begin{itemize}
  \item All set containers as a full subcategory;

  \item \(\cont{\mathbb 2}{\_.S^1}\), whose extent takes \(X\) to
    pairs of loops in \(X\);

  \item \(\cont{\FinSet}{\mathsf{\ustr{\_}}}\), where \(\FinSet\) is the
    (large) type of finite sets, \ie{} types that are merely
    equivalent to \(\Fin n\) for some \(n : \mathbb{N}\), as described
    in \cite[Section 5]{Joram2023}, and \(\ustr{\_}\) returns
    the underlying type. Assuming univalence, the
    loop space on a finite set is equivalent to the set of its
    automorphisms, so \(X \mapsto
    \settrunc{\ext{\cont{\FinSet}{\mathsf{El}}}\, X}\) represents the
    \emph{finite multiset} endofunctor.

\end{itemize}

\begin{section}{Monoids in \(\GpdCont\)}

  In~\cite{Uustalu2017} and resp. \cite{DePascalis2025} we
  characterise monoids in the monoidal category \((\Cont, {\csubst},
  \I)\) of \(\Set\) containers (resp. indexed \(\Set^I\) containers
  over a fixed set \(I\)), with container substitution and the
  identity container. In order to generalise this to the study of
  groupoid containers, we took the definition of a monoidal bicategory
  ~\cite[Section 12.1]{Johnson2020}, and adapted it to our locally
  univalent formulation. 

  We should now pick a suitable notion of monoid object. Pseudomonoids
  in a Gray-monoid are described in~\cite[Section 3]{Day1997}, and in
  classical higher category theory this definition suffices to define
  pseudomonoids in a monoidal bicategory, since classically, every
  monoidal bicategory is equivalent to a Gray-monoid. Expectably, to
  adapt this to our constructive locally univalent setting, we have to
  relax this definition to describe pseudomonoids in \(\GpdCont\), to
  obtain the notion of pseudomonoid in a (non-strict, non-univalent)
  \lutc{}.

  Formalisation of this part of locally univalent (2,1)-category
  theory is still underway. Nonetheless, we can already apply this
  definition of pseudomonoid in \((\GpdCont, \csubst, \I)\) to
  replicate the combinatorial unpacking seen in~\cite{Uustalu2017}.
  The resulting list of data and equations, though perhaps useful for
  formalisation or instance search purposes, is unwieldy for pen and
  paper presentation.

\end{section}

\begin{section}{Free monoids in \(\GpdCont\)}

  In classical category theory, it is well known that the free monad over a
  polynomial functor is itself polynomial~\cite{Gambino2009}. This translates to
  our setting as well.

  \begin{definition}
    Let \(F \defas \cont{S}{P}\) be a groupoid container. One can define a new
    container \(F^* \defas \cont{S^*}{P^*}\) by:
    \begin{align*}
      S^* & \defas \mu X \; . \; \Unit + \sum_{s : S} (P_s \to X) & {P^*}_{\inl \,*}     & \defas \Unit                       \\
          &                                                       & P^*_{\inr \, s\, s'} & \defas \sum_{p : P_s} P^*_{s'\, p}
    \end{align*}
  \end{definition}

  This container carries a pseudomonoid structure in the aforementioned sense,
  and it is our working hypothesis that it represents the free pseudomonad on
  \(\ext{F}\), as is the case for set containers. Furthermore, we are in the
  process of proving that it constitutes some sort of generalised list object
  (in the sense of ~\cite{Fiore2017}), for which the following inference rule
  holds:

  \[
    \begin{prooftree}
      \hypo{H \Rightarrow G}
      \hypo{G \csubst F \Rightarrow G}
      \infer2{H \csubst F^* \Rightarrow G}
    \end{prooftree}
    \qquad \text{for any fixed container \(H\)}
  \]

  Where \(H \Rightarrow G\) denotes container morphisms between \(H\) and \(G\).

\end{section}
